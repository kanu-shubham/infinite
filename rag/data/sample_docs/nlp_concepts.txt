Title: Natural Language Processing Concepts
Source: nlp_guide

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the field of computer science and linguistics concerned with enabling computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language. NLP combines computational linguistics with statistical and machine learning models to process text and speech at scale.

Tokenization is the process of splitting text into individual units called tokens. Word tokenization splits on whitespace and punctuation. Subword tokenization methods like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) and WordPiece learn a vocabulary of subword units from the training corpus, balancing vocabulary size with coverage. BPE starts with individual characters and iteratively merges the most frequent pairs. Most modern LLMs use subword tokenization — GPT models use BPE with a vocabulary of approximately 50,000 tokens, while BERT uses WordPiece with a vocabulary of 30,000 tokens.

Word Embeddings represent words as dense vectors in a continuous vector space where semantically similar words are close together. Word2Vec, introduced by Mikolov et al. at Google in 2013, trains shallow neural networks to predict a word from its context (CBOW) or predict context from a word (Skip-gram). GloVe (Global Vectors) uses matrix factorization on word co-occurrence statistics. Both produce static embeddings where each word has a single representation regardless of context. Contextual embeddings from models like BERT vary depending on the surrounding text, capturing polysemy (multiple meanings of a word).

Attention mechanisms were introduced to allow neural networks to focus on relevant parts of the input when generating each output token. Bahdanau et al. introduced additive attention in 2015 for sequence-to-sequence models. Scaled dot-product attention, used in Transformers, computes attention scores as (QK^T / sqrt(d_k)) and uses softmax to normalize them into a probability distribution over values V. The scaling by sqrt(d_k) prevents gradients from vanishing in the softmax when d_k is large.

Named Entity Recognition (NER) identifies and classifies entities in text into categories such as person names, organizations, locations, dates, and quantities. Modern NER systems use sequence labeling with token-level classification, typically with a CRF (Conditional Random Field) output layer on top of a transformer encoder. spaCy is a popular open-source NLP library that provides pretrained NER models for many languages.

Semantic Search differs from keyword search by finding documents that are semantically similar to a query rather than containing exact keyword matches. Dense retrieval encodes queries and documents as dense vectors and finds similar documents using approximate nearest neighbor search. Sparse retrieval methods like BM25 (Best Match 25) use term frequency and inverse document frequency statistics. BM25 extends TF-IDF with document length normalization and a saturation function for term frequency. Hybrid search combines dense and sparse retrieval scores, typically outperforming either method alone.

Question Answering (QA) is the task of automatically answering questions posed in natural language. Extractive QA finds the answer as a span within a retrieved document. Abstractive QA generates a new answer that may not appear verbatim in the source documents. Reading comprehension benchmarks like SQuAD (Stanford Question Answering Dataset) and HotpotQA (for multi-hop reasoning) are standard evaluation datasets. Multi-hop QA requires reasoning across multiple documents — for example, "What is the nationality of the director of Inception?" requires finding Inception's director (Christopher Nolan) and then his nationality (British-American).

Text summarization condenses long documents into shorter summaries. Extractive summarization selects and concatenates key sentences from the source. Abstractive summarization generates new text that paraphrases and condenses the source. ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) is the standard metric for evaluating summarization quality, measuring n-gram overlap between generated and reference summaries. BERTScore uses contextual BERT embeddings to evaluate semantic similarity between generated and reference text, correlating better with human judgments than ROUGE for abstractive summarization.
