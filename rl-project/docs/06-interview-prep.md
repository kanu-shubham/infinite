# 6. Interview Preparation

The questions below are the ones you will actually get. Answers are short on
purpose — pad them with the details from your project.

## 6.1 Fundamentals

**Q: What is reinforcement learning and how is it different from supervised learning?**
RL is sequential decision making. Instead of `(x, y)` pairs, the agent
interacts with an environment, receives a scalar reward, and learns a policy
that maximizes long-term reward. There are no labels; the "signal" is the
reward, which may be sparse and delayed.

**Q: What is an MDP?**
A tuple `(S, A, P, R, gamma)`: states, actions, transition probabilities,
reward function, discount. It formalizes problems with the **Markov property**:
the next state depends only on the current state and action.

**Q: Explain the Bellman equation.**
The recursive definition of value: `V(s) = E[r + gamma V(s')]`. It is the
foundation of every value-based RL algorithm.

**Q: Explain exploration vs exploitation. How did you handle it?**
Exploitation picks the best-known action; exploration tries new ones to
discover better actions. In DQN we used **epsilon-greedy with decay**
(start 1.0, end 0.05). In PPO exploration is implicit via the stochastic
policy plus an **entropy bonus** in the loss.

**Q: On-policy vs off-policy?**
On-policy learns from data generated by the current policy (PPO). Off-policy
can learn from any policy's data (DQN via replay buffer). Off-policy is more
sample-efficient but can be unstable.

## 6.2 DQN

**Q: Why a target network?**
The TD target `r + gamma max Q(s', a')` uses the same network being trained.
Without a separate, slowly-updated target, the target shifts every step and
the optimization becomes a moving target -> divergence.

**Q: Why a replay buffer?**
(a) It breaks temporal correlation between samples (iid assumption of SGD);
(b) it reuses past experience, improving sample efficiency.

**Q: What's the overestimation bias and how does Double DQN fix it?**
`max_a Q(s', a')` is biased upward because noise in Q is amplified by the max.
Double DQN uses the **online network to select** the action and the
**target network to evaluate** it, decoupling the two and reducing the bias.

**Q: When would DQN fail?**
Continuous or very high-dim discrete action spaces, very long horizons with
sparse rewards, or when the Markov property is violated badly.

## 6.3 PPO

**Q: Why policy gradients?**
They naturally handle continuous actions, learn stochastic policies, and
avoid the `argmax` bottleneck. PPO adds stability via the clipped objective.

**Q: Why clip the ratio?**
To prevent catastrophically large policy updates. Clipping bounds how much the
new policy can drift from the old one per update — a cheap approximation to
TRPO's trust region.

**Q: What is GAE and why use it?**
Generalized Advantage Estimation blends Monte Carlo and TD(0) using a single
`lambda` parameter. It trades off bias (TD) vs variance (MC). `lambda=0.95`
is the usual choice.

**Q: What's the entropy bonus for?**
Keeps the policy stochastic enough to explore and prevents premature
convergence to a deterministic, possibly suboptimal, policy.

## 6.4 Your Project (Inventory)

**Q: Walk me through your project.**
"I formulated warehouse inventory management as an MDP. The **state** is
current stock, pending orders, day of week, and rolling demand statistics.
The **action** is how many units to order. The **reward** is daily profit
minus holding and stockout costs. I implemented **DQN** (discrete order
quantities) and **PPO** (continuous). Both were trained against a stochastic
demand simulator and evaluated against a tuned (s, S) baseline. PPO
outperformed the baseline by ~12% cumulative profit on held-out demand
sequences and generalized better to non-stationary demand."

**Q: How did you design the reward?**
"Dollar-denominated so I can show a CFO: `sales_revenue - order_cost -
holding_cost - stockout_penalty`. I was careful with the stockout penalty —
too low, the agent ignores customers; too high, it over-orders. I tuned it to
match the business' estimated lifetime-value loss from a stockout."

**Q: How did you validate it?**
"Held out demand sequences the agent never saw during training. Reported
profit, stockout rate, and average holding cost. Compared to three baselines
(do nothing, newsvendor, tuned (s, S))."

**Q: How would you deploy this in production?**
"Offline training on historical demand, simulator fit from data, policy
wrapped in safety rules (capacity caps, budget caps), shadow-mode deployment
first, then A/B test against incumbent rules. Continuous monitoring of state-
action-reward and alerts on distribution drift for retraining."

**Q: What would break in production?**
"Non-stationarity beyond what the training data captured (new competitor,
supply chain shock). I'd plan for periodic retraining and have a fallback
rule-based controller the system can switch to automatically."

## 6.5 ML Systems

**Q: How do you monitor an RL system?**
Log `(state, action, reward, next_state)` tuples. Track rolling episode
return, action distribution (KL from training-time), and business KPIs. Alert
on drift.

**Q: How do you handle safety?**
(i) Action masking / clipping for hard constraints; (ii) shield policies;
(iii) offline evaluation before deployment; (iv) human-in-the-loop in early
stages; (v) rollback plans.

**Q: Offline RL vs online RL?**
Online interacts with the environment. Offline learns purely from logged
data (no new interaction). Offline needs conservative algorithms (CQL, BCQ)
to avoid exploiting out-of-distribution actions.

## 6.6 Trap Questions

**Q: Your agent got high reward — does that mean success?**
Not necessarily. Check for **reward hacking** (exploiting a reward flaw) and
**overfitting to the simulator**. Always evaluate on held-out scenarios and
against real-world KPIs.

**Q: Why not just use supervised learning on historical decisions?**
Because historical decisions are the **output of an old, suboptimal policy**.
Imitating them caps performance at human/rule level. RL can exceed it.
